Why chips are printed with ultraviolet light

A lens can’t focus light to a point. It makes a small blur, the point spread function, whose bright core has radius r = 0.61 λ / NA. To draw finer lines you need a shorter wavelength λ, a larger numerical aperture NA, or both. The animation shows every generation of chip-making light at the same 2 µm scale.

When can you tell two points apart?

Two equally bright points, each blurred into its own PSF. Lord Rayleigh’s rule: they are resolved when one peak sits on the other’s first dark ring, a separation of exactly one Airy radius. Here the two points stay put while you change the wavelength: shorter light shrinks each blur until the pair splits apart. The F-number follows the explorer above.

Sources give the Rayleigh midpoint as 73.5% of the peaks or a 26.3% dip; the exact Bessel calculation on this page gives 73.5%. Sparrow’s limit, 0.47 λ/NA, is 0.77 Airy radii.

Printing lines on a chip

A scanner images a mask of lines onto a light-sensitive resist. The finest half-pitch it can print is CD = k₁ · λ / NA. Physics stops k₁ at 0.25: below that, the line pattern is finer than the lens can pass, and the image on the wafer is perfectly flat grey. So the industry shortened λ and pushed NA up, generation after generation.

Smallest half-pitch each generation can print at k₁ = 0.25 (log scale), with its depth of focus ≈ λ/NA²

These are physical limits. Production runs at higher k₁, typically about 0.4: EUV scanners are specified at 13 nm and High-NA EUV at 8 nm, about k₁ ≈ 0.32. For ArF immersion at NA 1.35 the 36 nm limit matches the published figure.

Shorter wavelength

Mercury lamps gave 436 and 365 nm. Excimer lasers brought 248 nm (KrF) and 193 nm (ArF). EUV jumps to 13.5 nm from a tin plasma; every material absorbs it, so the optics become mirrors in vacuum.

Bigger NA, even above 1

NA = n·sin θ. Dry lenses top out near 0.93. Putting water (n ≈ 1.44 at 193 nm) between the last lens and the wafer lets tools reach NA 1.35, a trick called immersion lithography.

The price: depth of focus

Depth of focus falls as 1/NA², much faster than resolution improves. By the λ/NA² estimate, High-NA EUV focuses within tens of nanometres, so wafers must be extremely flat.

The same physics in your living room

CD, DVD and Blu-ray store data in spiral tracks read by a focused laser spot. A smaller spot allows tighter tracks, so each generation used a shorter wavelength and a higher NA. All three are drawn at the same 4 µm scale, with the track spacing dashed.

Microscopes hit the same wall: with oil immersion (NA 1.4) and green light (550 nm), the Airy radius is about 240 nm, which is why ordinary light microscopes can’t resolve finer detail without super-resolution tricks.

    How it is computed: the PSF is the Airy pattern [2J₁(v)/v]² with v = 2π·NA·r/λ, for an aberration-free circular pupil. Airy radius r = 0.61 λ/NA = 1.22 λ·F/#, with NA = 1/(2·F/#). Line images use ideal dipole illumination: the mask’s diffraction orders that fit inside the pupil interfere, and none beyond the first can pass once half-pitch < λ/(4NA), the k₁ = 0.25 limit. Real scanners add finite pole sizes, resist chemistry and other enhancement tricks; the limit is the same, the details differ.

    Numbers: wavelengths are standard source lines; NA values are the highest published for each generation. Depth of focus uses k₂ = 1 in DOF = k₂ λ/NA².
